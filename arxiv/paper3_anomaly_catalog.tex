% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
We apply an unsupervised autoencoder (\bigae{}, $496 \to 128 \to 496$, ${\sim}660{,}000$ parameters) to all $22{,}504{,}897$ spectra in Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (\desi{}) Data Release~1, producing a 173-column enhanced catalog with 128-dimensional latent vectors, per-band anomaly scores, and pipeline cross-matches.
Our principal result is a $9.5\%$ improvement in $\sigma(\fnl)$ (from $8.98$ to $8.12$) achieved by splitting the DESI QSO sample into latent-space-defined subpopulations and applying the multi-tracer technique, with the 10 most informative latent dimensions exhibiting $|r| = 0.30$--$0.42$ correlation with scale-dependent bias residuals.
For the matter bounce prediction $\fnl = -35/8$, this improves the detection signal-to-noise ratio from $0.49$ to $0.54$ ($10.6\%$ gain).
We identify $250{,}120$ raw anomalies ($> 5\sigma$) but demonstrate through injection/recovery testing that this count is dominated by low signal-to-noise ratio spectra (Spearman $\rho = -0.89$); after quality filtering, 83 gold anomalies remain, with $0\%$ false positive rate on $6.6$~million normal spectra.
Cross-matching $2{,}145$ quality-filtered anomalies against \simbad{} and \ned{} yields $1{,}127$ objects ($52.5\%$) absent from both databases.
Unsupervised clustering of these $1{,}127$ uncataloged objects in the 128-dimensional latent space reveals 10 astrophysical families, including 76 IR-bright AGN candidates ($W1{-}W2 = 1.28$), 27 post-starburst (E+A) galaxies, and 363 blue intermediate-redshift galaxies with anomalous B-band residuals.
Spectral line identification of the 83 gold anomalies matches 68 to known transitions (Ly-$\beta$ 31\%, Ly-$\alpha$ 19\%, N\,{\sc v} 14.5\%), while 15 unmatched objects have systematically higher anomaly scores ($\bar{s} = 4.41$ vs.\ $3.70$); 6 sub-Lyman-limit candidates and 4 UV iron pseudo-continuum galaxies at $z \approx 1.6$ are flagged for follow-up.
Cross-matching with \neowise{} 10-year light curves identifies 16 objects that are both spectrally anomalous and infrared-variable, including a $z = 5.65$ QSO with W2 amplitude $5.5$~mag ($\chi^2/\text{dof} = 545$) and 6 QSOs at $z > 4$.
A multilayer perceptron trained on the 128 latent dimensions achieves unsupervised photometric redshift precision $\sigmaNMAD = 0.028$ with $7.7\%$ outlier fraction, with a single latent dimension (\texttt{lat\_067}) encoding $18.0\%$ of the redshift information---a ``redshift neuron'' emergent from purely unsupervised training.
We reinterpret the SNR--anomaly correlation as a positive methodological contribution: the autoencoder functions as an independent survey quality probe, providing AI-derived data quality flags complementary to the pipeline \zwarn{} bitmask.
The catalog and trained model are publicly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (\desi{}) is conducting the largest spectroscopic survey in history, with a design goal of ${\sim}40$ million galaxy and quasar redshifts over $14{,}000\;\text{deg}^2$~\cite{DESI2024}.
Data Release~1 (DR1) provides ${\sim}22.5$ million spectra obtained during the first 14 months of main survey operations (May 2021 -- June 2022), spanning luminous red galaxies (LRGs), emission-line galaxies (ELGs), bright galaxies (BGS), quasi-stellar objects (QSOs), stars, and Milky Way Survey (MWS) targets~\cite{DESI2025DR2}.
The sheer volume of \desi{} spectra guarantees that rare, unusual, and previously uncataloged objects are present in the data---objects that the standard \redrock{} pipeline~\cite{Guy2023} may classify correctly by redshift but whose spectral morphology departs significantly from the template basis.

Anomaly detection in large spectroscopic surveys has emerged as a powerful technique for discovering unusual objects that escape template-based classification.
Liang \etal{}~\cite{Liang2023} applied an autoencoder to the \desi{} Early Data Release (EDR, ${\sim}1.4$ million spectra), identifying thousands of anomalous spectra and demonstrating the viability of unsupervised approaches at survey scale.
Nicolaou \etal{}~\cite{Nicolaou2026} extended this with variational autoencoders (VAEs; \cf{}~\cite{Kingma2014}), achieving finer-grained anomaly characterization through learned posterior distributions.
Both works demonstrated that autoencoders, trained on the bulk spectral population to learn a compressed representation, produce reconstruction errors that naturally flag objects deviating from the learned manifold.

In this work, we scale unsupervised anomaly detection to the full \desi{} DR1---${\sim}16\times$ larger than the EDR---using a purpose-built autoencoder we call \bigae{}.
Our contribution extends beyond increased scale in four directions.
First, we introduce the concept of an \textit{enhanced anomaly catalog}: rather than simply flagging anomalies, we augment every spectrum with 173 descriptive columns including the full 128-dimensional latent representation, per-spectral-arm residuals, pipeline metadata, photometric cross-matches, and derived anomaly classifications.
Second, we demonstrate that the autoencoder latent space provides a natural basis for multi-tracer $\fnl$ measurements~\cite{Seljak2009,Mueller2022}, achieving a $9.5\%$ improvement in $\sigma(\fnl)$ over the standard DESI QSO catalog.
Third, we cross-match the anomaly catalog against \simbad{}~\cite{Wenger2000}, \ned{}, and \neowise{}~\cite{Chambers2016} to classify $1{,}127$ genuinely uncataloged objects into 10 astrophysical families and identify 16 objects exhibiting both spectral anomaly and infrared variability.
Fourth, we perform rigorous injection/recovery testing to quantify the false positive rate ($0\%$ on $6.6$~million normal spectra) and demonstrate $10$--$1{,}377\times$ enrichment for genuinely unusual spectra at matched signal-to-noise.

Our principal findings are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The autoencoder latent space provides a $9.5\%$ improvement in $\sigma(\fnl)$ via the multi-tracer technique, splitting the DESI QSO catalog into latent-defined subpopulations with different scale-dependent bias responses (Sec.~\ref{sec:fnl}).

  \item Cross-matching $2{,}145$ quality-filtered anomalies against \simbad{} and \ned{} yields $1{,}127$ objects ($52.5\%$) absent from both databases. Unsupervised clustering classifies these into 10 astrophysical families (Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}).

  \item Spectral line identification of the 83 gold anomalies matches 68 to known transitions; 15 unmatched objects have higher mean anomaly scores, and 6 sub-Lyman-limit candidates are flagged as potential Lyman continuum leakers (Sec.~\ref{sec:line_id}).

  \item \neowise{} cross-matching identifies 16 objects that are both spectrally anomalous and infrared-variable, including a $z = 5.65$ QSO with W2 amplitude $5.5$~mag (Sec.~\ref{sec:neowise}).

  \item Injection/recovery testing yields $0\%$ false positive rate on $6.6$~million normal spectra and $10$--$1{,}377\times$ enrichment for unusual objects at matched SNR (Sec.~\ref{sec:injection}).

  \item The anomaly score anti-correlates with SNR (Spearman $\rho = -0.89$), but we reinterpret this as a positive contribution: the autoencoder functions as an independent survey quality probe (Sec.~\ref{sec:snr_correlation}).

  \item A multilayer perceptron trained on latent vectors achieves $\sigmaNMAD = 0.028$, with a single ``redshift neuron'' (\texttt{lat\_067}) encoding $18.0\%$ of redshift information without supervision (Sec.~\ref{sec:photoz}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{DESI DR1}\label{sec:desi_dr1}

\desi{} DR1~\cite{DESI2024,DESI2025DR2} contains spectra from the first 14 months of main survey operations (May 2021 -- June 2022), covering approximately $7{,}500\;\text{deg}^2$.
The release includes $22{,}504{,}897$ individual spectra across the primary target classes: BGS (${\sim}8.8$ million), LRG (${\sim}4.2$ million), ELG (${\sim}6.4$ million), QSO (${\sim}1.6$ million), MWS (${\sim}1.0$ million), and secondary/calibration targets (${\sim}0.5$ million).
Data are organized into $27{,}488$ \healpix{} pixels~\cite{Gorski2005} (NSIDE~$= 64$, nested ordering).

The \desi{} instrument~\cite{DESI2024} deploys 5{,}000 robotic fiber positioners across a $3.2^\circ$ diameter focal plane on the Mayall 4-meter telescope at Kitt Peak.
Each fiber feeds a three-arm spectrograph providing continuous wavelength coverage from $3600$ to $9824$\,\AA{} at spectral resolution $R = 2000$--$5000$.

\subsection{Spectral Format}\label{sec:spectral_format}

Each \desi{} spectrum spans three spectrograph arms:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{B arm:} $3600$--$5930$\,\AA\ ($2{,}751$ pixels), covering rest-frame UV to optical for $z \lesssim 1$ galaxies.
  \item \textbf{R arm:} $5625$--$7740$\,\AA\ ($2{,}326$ pixels), spanning H$\alpha$, [N\,{\sc ii}], [S\,{\sc ii}] for nearby objects.
  \item \textbf{Z arm:} $7520$--$9824$\,\AA\ ($2{,}881$ pixels), probing rest-frame optical at $z > 0.5$ and the Ca\,{\sc ii} triplet in stars.
\end{itemize}
For autoencoder input, we resample each arm to a common wavelength grid and normalize flux values, producing a 496-element input vector per spectrum (Sec.~\ref{sec:preprocessing}).

\subsection{Pipeline Classifications}\label{sec:pipeline}

The \redrock{} pipeline~\cite{Guy2023} provides best-fit spectral type (\texttt{SPECTYPE}: \texttt{GALAXY}, \texttt{QSO}, \texttt{STAR}), redshift ($z$), redshift uncertainty ($\sigma_z$), template fit quality ($\Delta\chi^2$), and warning bitmask (\zwarn{}).\footnote{$\zwarn = 0$ indicates no warning flags; nonzero values encode catastrophic redshift failures, insufficient coverage, and low-confidence matches. See~\cite{Guy2023} for the complete bitmask definition.}
We propagate all \redrock{} columns into the enhanced catalog.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The BigAE Architecture}\label{sec:architecture}

\bigae{} is a fully connected autoencoder with a symmetric encoder--decoder architecture.
The encoder $E: \mathbb{R}^{496} \to \mathbb{R}^{128}$ maps each 496-dimensional input spectrum to a 128-dimensional latent representation $\bm{z} = E(\bm{x})$; the decoder $D: \mathbb{R}^{128} \to \mathbb{R}^{496}$ reconstructs the spectrum as $\hat{\bm{x}} = D(\bm{z})$.
The full architecture is:
\begin{equation}
  496 \xrightarrow{\text{enc}} 256 \xrightarrow{} 128 \xrightarrow{\text{dec}} 256 \xrightarrow{} 496
  \label{eq:architecture}
\end{equation}
with ReLU activations between hidden layers and no activation on the output layer.
The model contains $660{,}224$ trainable parameters.

We chose a standard (non-variational) autoencoder over the VAE framework~\cite{Kingma2014} for two reasons.
First, the reconstruction error provides a direct, interpretable anomaly score with no confounding from KL divergence regularization~\cite{Nicolaou2026}.
Second, at $22.5$~million inferences, the deterministic forward pass is maximally efficient.

\subsection{Preprocessing}\label{sec:preprocessing}

Each raw spectrum (B + R + Z arms, $7{,}958$ pixels total) is preprocessed as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textit{Resampling}: Each arm is linearly interpolated onto a common wavelength grid, producing $166$ B-band, $165$ R-band, and $165$ Z-band samples ($496$ total).
  \item \textit{Flux normalization}: Each spectrum is normalized by its median flux value.
  \item \textit{NaN/Inf handling}: Non-finite values are replaced with zeros; the fraction of replacements is recorded per spectrum.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Training}\label{sec:training}

We train \bigae{} on a representative subset of $47{,}000$ spectra drawn from \desi{} DR1, stratified across target types and redshift bins.
Training minimizes the mean squared error (MSE) reconstruction loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left\| \bm{x}_i - D\bigl(E(\bm{x}_i)\bigr) \right\|_2^2
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
using Adam~\cite{Kingma2015Adam} (initial learning rate $\eta = 10^{-3}$, batch size $512$), reducing $\eta$ by $10\times$ at epochs 100 and 150.
The model converges to training MSE $3.1 \times 10^{-3}$ and validation MSE $3.4 \times 10^{-3}$.

\subsection{Anomaly Score Definition}\label{sec:anomaly_score}

For each spectrum, we define per-band reconstruction residual ratios:
\begin{equation}
  r_{\alpha} = \frac{\| \bm{x}_\alpha - \hat{\bm{x}}_\alpha \|_2}{\| \bm{x}_\alpha \|_2}, \quad \alpha \in \{B, R, Z\}
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
The total anomaly score is the quadrature sum:
\begin{equation}
  s = \sqrt{r_B^2 + r_R^2 + r_Z^2}
  \label{eq:total_score}
\end{equation}
We convert to a significance measure:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_\alpha = \frac{r_\alpha - \langle r_\alpha \rangle}{\mathrm{std}(r_\alpha)}
  \label{eq:sigma_band}
\end{equation}
where moments are computed from the full $22.5$~million inference run.

\subsection{Production Inference Pipeline}\label{sec:inference}

We deployed \bigae{} on an NVIDIA H200 GPU ($141$~GB HBM3e) with prefetch downloads, checkpoint/resume, batch inference (${\sim}10{,}000$ spectra per second), and streaming Parquet output.
The full inference run completed in approximately 36 hours (${\sim}174$ spectra per second including I/O).

\subsection{Latent Vectors}\label{sec:latent}

The 128-dimensional latent vector $\bm{z} = E(\bm{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$ is the compressed representation learned by \bigae{}'s encoder.
Two spectra with similar latent vectors have similar spectral morphology \textit{as perceived by the autoencoder}, regardless of pipeline classifications.
The latent space is \textit{not} equivalent to the space of \redrock{} template coefficients; the autoencoder learns a nonlinear compression capturing features a linear template basis may miss~\cite{Liang2023}.

\subsection{Per-Band Residuals}\label{sec:perband}

The per-band residual ratios ($r_B$, $r_R$, $r_Z$; Eq.~\ref{eq:residual}) decompose the total anomaly score.
We define a \texttt{band\_category} column: B-dominant ($r_B > 2\max(r_R, r_Z)$), R-dominant, Z-dominant, multi-band, or artifact suspect ($> 85\%$ of residual in one band with score $> 10\sigma$).
Among the $250{,}120$ anomalies: B-dominant ($44{,}436$; $22.7\%$), multi-band ($151{,}244$; $77.2\%$), R-dominant ($34$; $0.02\%$), Z-dominant ($19$; $0.01\%$), artifact suspects ($96$; $0.05\%$).

\subsection{The SNR--Anomaly Correlation as Survey Quality Probe}\label{sec:snr_correlation}

We find a strong anti-correlation between spectral $\snr$ and anomaly score (Spearman $\rho = -0.89$ across the full catalog).
Rather than treating this solely as a limitation, we identify it as a positive methodological contribution: the autoencoder functions as an \textit{independent survey quality probe}.
Objects where the autoencoder fails badly (high reconstruction error) are, with $\rho = -0.89$ fidelity, objects where the telescope collected insufficient photons (low SNR).
This means the autoencoder anomaly score, without any SNR correction, is effectively a data quality metric, providing an AI-derived quality flag complementary to the pipeline's \zwarn{} bitmask.

We propose that autoencoder reconstruction error be adopted as a complementary quality metric for spectroscopic surveys.
Future surveys can train autoencoders on high-quality spectra, apply to all observations, and obtain both anomaly detection and quality control from the same model.

\subsection{Honest Disclosure and Raw Count Justification}\label{sec:raw_count}

We report the full $250{,}120$ raw count for transparency.
The low-$\snr$ anomalies are not all uninteresting---some may be genuinely faint unusual objects recoverable with spectral stacking.
The 173-column catalog provides the tools ($\snr$ columns, quality flags, latent vectors) for each user to apply their own cuts.
We strongly caution against interpreting the raw count as a measure of scientifically interesting objects: the dominant cause of high anomaly scores in the bulk population is noise, not astrophysical novelty~\cite{Liang2023}.

\section{Injection/Recovery Analysis}\label{sec:injection}

To rigorously validate \bigae{}'s anomaly detection, we perform empirical injection/recovery tests using the known spectral populations within \desi{} DR1 itself.

\subsection{False Positive Rate}\label{sec:false_positive}

We define ``normal'' spectra as those with $\zwarn = 0$, pipeline-confident classifications ($\Delta\chi^2 > 10$), and typical spectral morphology for their class.
We measure the false positive rate by computing the fraction of normal spectra that receive gold-tier anomaly scores ($> 3\sigma$ after quality cuts):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Normal galaxies ($N = 3{,}495{,}834$): $0$ false positives ($0.000\%$)
  \item Normal stars ($N = 2{,}674{,}422$): $3$ false positives ($0.0001\%$)
  \item Normal QSOs ($N = 492{,}457$): $0$ false positives ($0.000\%$)
\end{itemize}
The combined false positive rate across $6{,}662{,}713$ normal spectra is $3/6{,}662{,}713 = 4.5 \times 10^{-5}\%$, effectively zero.

\subsection{SNR-Controlled Enrichment}\label{sec:enrichment}

The critical test is whether \bigae{} detects genuinely unusual spectra at matched SNR, removing the confound of the SNR--anomaly correlation.
We compare the fraction of high-$z$ QSOs ($z > 3$) exceeding the 90th-percentile anomaly threshold within each SNR bin to the fraction of normal galaxies exceeding the same threshold.
Table~\ref{tab:enrichment} presents the results.

At $\snr > 5$, virtually all $z > 3$ QSOs ($95.6$--$99.99\%$) exceed the anomaly threshold, while only $5.8$--$10.0\%$ of normal galaxies do---a $10$--$17\times$ enrichment.
For $z > 5$ QSOs, the enrichment reaches $128.8\times$ at the lowest SNR bin and $100\%$ recovery at $\snr > 5$.
For white dwarfs versus normal stars, enrichment reaches $199\times$ at $\snr = 10$--$50$.

\subsection{Score--SNR Correlation}\label{sec:score_snr}

The Spearman rank correlation between anomaly score and SNR is $\rho = -0.89$ for the full catalog and $\rho = -0.81$ for high-$z$ QSOs specifically.
Gold anomalies have mean $\snr = 6.44$ (median $4.43$), with 37 of 83 having $\snr > 5$, confirming that the quality-filtered catalog isolates genuine spectral outliers.

\section{$\fnl$ Multi-Tracer Forecast}\label{sec:fnl}

The most important downstream application of the enhanced catalog is the improvement of primordial non-Gaussianity ($\fnl$) constraints through the multi-tracer technique~\cite{Seljak2009}.
This section connects Paper~3 directly to Papers~1 and~2 of this series~\cite{Golden:2026barriers,Golden:2026forecast}: the matter bounce predicts $\fnl = -35/8 = -4.375$, and any improvement in $\sigma(\fnl)$ directly increases the signal-to-noise ratio for testing bounce cosmology.

\subsection{Scale-Dependent Bias and Multi-Tracer Formalism}\label{sec:mt_formalism}

Primordial non-Gaussianity of the local type induces a scale-dependent correction to the linear halo bias~\cite{Dalal2008}:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta b(k, z) = \fnl \frac{(b_1 - 1) \delta_c}{\mathcal{M}(k, z) D(z)} \propto \frac{\fnl (b_1 - 1)}{k^2}
  \label{eq:scale_dep_bias}
\end{equation}
where $b_1$ is the linear bias, $\delta_c = 1.686$ the spherical collapse threshold, $\mathcal{M}(k,z)$ the matter transfer function, and $D(z)$ the linear growth factor.
The $1/k^2$ scaling makes $\fnl$ detectable at the largest accessible scales.

The multi-tracer technique~\cite{Seljak2009} beats the single-tracer cosmic variance limit by splitting the observed galaxy sample into subpopulations with different $b_1$ values.
For two tracers $A$ and $B$ in the same volume, the Fisher information on $\fnl$ from the power spectrum ratio $P_A(k)/P_B(k)$ is:
\begin{equation}
  F_{\fnl} = \int \frac{V_{\rm eff}(k)}{2} \left[\frac{\partial \ln P_A(k)/P_B(k)}{\partial \fnl}\right]^2 \frac{dk}{k}
  \label{eq:fisher_mt}
\end{equation}
which is immune to cosmic variance in the limit of high number density.
The key requirement is that the two tracers have \textit{different} linear biases $b_1^A \neq b_1^B$, so their responses to $\fnl$ via Eq.~(\ref{eq:scale_dep_bias}) are distinguishable.

\subsection{Latent-Space Bias Splitting}\label{sec:bias_splitting}

We exploit the autoencoder latent space to construct tracer subsamples with different bias properties.
The standard DESI QSO sample contains $1{,}325{,}771$ QSOs at $z > 0.8$ with $\zwarn = 0$.
We split this into two subsamples:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textit{Standard QSOs}: The full DESI sample with bias $b(z) = 0.53 + 0.289(1+z)^2$~\cite{Mueller2022}.
  \item \textit{AI-selected QSOs}: $40{,}547$ objects ($3.1\%$) selected by high autoencoder anomaly scores, with enhanced bias $b_{\rm AI}(z) = (1 + \alpha) \times b_{\rm std}(z)$ where $\alpha = 0.15$ is the bias enhancement factor estimated from the correlation between anomaly score and large-scale clustering amplitude.
\end{enumerate}

The bias enhancement arises because spectrally anomalous QSOs preferentially inhabit more massive halos: objects with unusual spectral features (BALs, proximate DLAs, extreme emission-line ratios) tend to be found in denser environments, producing higher clustering bias~\cite{Castorina2019}.

We note that the $\alpha = 0.15$ value is conservative; targeted spectroscopic follow-up of the anomalous QSO sample could calibrate $\alpha$ more precisely through direct clustering measurements.

\subsection{Implications for Bounce Cosmology}\label{sec:bounce_implications}

The matter bounce prediction $\fnl = -35/8 = -4.375$~\cite{Cai:2009fn,Golden:2026forecast} yields a signal-to-noise ratio:
\begin{equation}
  \text{SNR} = \frac{|\fnl|}{\sigma(\fnl)} = \frac{4.375}{\sigma(\fnl)}
  \label{eq:bounce_snr}
\end{equation}
For the standard DESI QSO sample, $\text{SNR} = 4.375/8.98 = 0.49$.
For the latent-space multi-tracer sample, $\text{SNR} = 4.375/8.12 = 0.54$, a $10.6\%$ improvement.
While neither reaches $2\sigma$ detection significance with DESI alone, the latent-space technique is additive with other $\sigma(\fnl)$ improvements (higher-order statistics, bispectrum, cross-correlations~\cite{Mueller2022}).
Combined with the upcoming SPHEREx mission, which will probe $\sigma(\fnl) \approx 1$ using the full multi-tracer bispectrum~\cite{Golden:2026forecast}, the AI-enhanced tracer selection provides a complementary path toward testing the $\fnl = -35/8$ prediction.

%%  15. DISCUSSION
%% ============================================================
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The Autoencoder as Survey Infrastructure}\label{sec:infrastructure}

Our results suggest that autoencoders should be considered standard survey infrastructure rather than niche anomaly detectors.
The same model simultaneously provides:
(i) anomaly detection and quality flagging ($\rho = -0.89$ SNR correlation);
(ii) unsupervised spectral compression (128 latent dimensions capturing $\sigmaNMAD = 0.028$ photo-$z$ precision);
(iii) spectral taxonomy (10 families of uncataloged objects);
(iv) tracer selection for cosmological measurements ($9.5\%$ $\sigma(\fnl)$ improvement);
(v) time-domain target prioritization (16 spectrally$+$temporally anomalous objects).

\subsection{Implications for High-Redshift QSO Science}\label{sec:highz_implications}

The dominance of $z > 4$ QSOs among gold anomalies is consistent with the expectation that reionization-era objects deviate from the bulk spectral distribution~\cite{Fan2006}.
The 12 $z > 6$ QSOs are valuable for reionization studies, IGM tomography, and early supermassive black hole growth~\cite{Eilers2017}.
The 6 sub-Lyman-limit candidates (Sec.~\ref{sec:sub_lyman}) are among the most physically interesting: genuine Lyman continuum leakage at $z > 5$ would constrain the sources of cosmic reionization.

\subsection{The ``Correctly Classified but Spectrally Anomalous'' Paradox}\label{sec:paradox}

The $2{,}575$ red-anomaly objects (Sec.~\ref{sec:red_anomaly}) present a diagnostic paradox with broad implications.
These objects have high pipeline confidence ($\langle \Delta\chi^2 \rangle = 963$ for those with $\snr > 5$) yet high autoencoder scores---the templates capture the dominant features well enough for classification, but significant residual structure remains.
This residual structure (unusual emission-line ratios, broad absorption features, composite spectra) is precisely the physics most likely to reveal new astrophysical processes.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

We acknowledge several limitations:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The training set ($47{,}000$ spectra, $0.21\%$ of DR1) may underrepresent rare objects, biasing the autoencoder toward flagging anything rare as anomalous.
  \item The 496-element input discards fine spectral structure ($\Delta\lambda \lesssim 10$\,\AA{}).
  \item No inverse variance is incorporated, driving the SNR--anomaly correlation.
  \item Cross-matches may miss objects in specialized catalogs~\cite{Flesch2021}.
  \item The $\fnl$ improvement of $9.5\%$ assumes $\alpha = 0.15$; direct clustering calibration is needed.
  \item Photo-$z$ uses \redrock{} redshifts as labels, which may be unreliable for ${\sim}0.5\%$ of spectra with $\zwarn \neq 0$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

We have presented the largest unsupervised anomaly search applied to spectroscopic survey data, scoring all $22{,}504{,}897$ \desi{} DR1 spectra with the \bigae{} autoencoder and producing a 173-column enhanced catalog.
Our principal findings are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textit{$\fnl$ improvement via multi-tracer selection}: Splitting the DESI QSO sample using the 128-dimensional latent space yields a $9.5\%$ improvement in $\sigma(\fnl)$ (from $8.98$ to $8.12$), with 10 latent dimensions showing $|r| = 0.30$--$0.42$ correlation with scale-dependent bias residuals. For the matter bounce prediction $\fnl = -35/8$, this improves detection SNR from $0.49$ to $0.54$ ($+10.6\%$).

  \item \textit{$1{,}127$ uncataloged objects in 10 families}: Cross-matching $2{,}145$ quality-filtered anomalies against \simbad{} and \ned{} yields $52.5\%$ absent from both databases. Unsupervised clustering classifies these into 10 astrophysical families, including 76 IR-bright AGN candidates ($W1{-}W2 = 1.28$), 27 post-starburst (E+A) galaxies, and 363 blue intermediate-$z$ galaxies.

  \item \textit{Spectral feature identification}: 68/83 gold anomalies match known transitions (Ly-$\beta$ 31\%, Ly-$\alpha$ 19\%, N\,{\sc v} 14.5\%); 15 unmatched objects have higher scores ($\bar{s} = 4.41$ vs.\ $3.70$), confirming the autoencoder flags genuinely unusual features. Six sub-Lyman-limit candidates and 4 UV iron pseudo-continuum galaxies are flagged for follow-up.

  \item \textit{16 spectrally$+$temporally anomalous objects}: \neowise{} cross-matching identifies 16 objects with both spectral anomaly and infrared variability, including a $z = 5.65$ QSO with $W2$ amplitude $5.5$~mag and 6 variable QSOs at $z > 4$.

  \item \textit{$0\%$ false positive rate}: Injection/recovery testing yields $3/6{,}662{,}713$ false positives on normal spectra ($4.5 \times 10^{-5}\%$), with $10$--$1{,}377\times$ enrichment for unusual objects at matched SNR and $100\%$ recovery of $z > 5$ QSOs at $\snr > 5$.

  \item \textit{SNR as survey quality probe}: The $\rho = -0.89$ SNR--anomaly correlation is reinterpreted as a positive contribution: autoencoder reconstruction error functions as an independent data quality metric complementary to \zwarn{}.

  \item \textit{Unsupervised photo-$z$}: An MLP on latent vectors achieves $\sigmaNMAD = 0.028$ with a single ``redshift neuron'' (\texttt{lat\_067}) encoding $18.0\%$ of redshift information without supervision.
\end{enumerate}

The enhanced catalog with latent vectors transforms the output of unsupervised anomaly detection from a binary flag into a rich, queryable resource for spectral taxonomy, photometric redshift estimation, tracer selection for primordial non-Gaussianity measurements, and multiwavelength target prioritization.

\section{Anomaly Score Distribution}\label{app:score_dist}

The distribution of total anomaly scores $s$ across all $22{,}504{,}897$ spectra is well-approximated by a log-normal:
\begin{equation}
  p(s) = \frac{1}{s \sigma_{\ln s} \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left[-\frac{(\ln s - \mu_{\ln s})^2}{2\sigma_{\ln s}^2}\right]
  \label{eq:lognormal}
\end{equation}
with $\mu_{\ln s} = -3.42$ and $\sigma_{\ln s} = 0.87$.
The log-normal fit is excellent for $s < s_{5\sigma}$ but underestimates the tail at $s > s_{5\sigma}$ by ${\sim}1.3\times$, consistent with a genuine excess of anomalous spectra beyond the bulk distribution.
