\title{A provenance-sealed DESI DR1 anomaly-score candidate catalogue: selection,
validation, and a null known-object recovery benchmark}

\author{Houston Golden}
\email{houston@hubify.com}
\affiliation{Hubify Research}

\date{\paperTimestamp\ (\paperVersion)}

\begin{abstract}
No discovery is claimed in this paper: we publish a machine-flagged candidate
catalogue of spectroscopically unusual objects in Data Release 1 of the Dark
Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), together with the complete provenance
chain needed to reproduce the catalogue's selection (score, provenance gate,
threshold), and a known-object recovery benchmark that clears none of its
pre-declared bars. One downstream, non-selection analysis --- the descriptive
taxonomy of Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy} --- is fully specified and reproducible
from the released files (spherical sky embedding, stated hyperparameters and
seed), carrying its own separate provenance record distinct from the sealed
selection chain (Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}). An archived convolutional autoencoder,
bound by SHA-256 to a sealed run contract, assigns a reconstruction-error
anomaly score $S$ to every science-target spectrum in the public
\texttt{iron} release; $28{,}425{,}963$ raw rows deduplicate to
$27{,}547{,}223$ unique \TARGETID s, of which $21{,}793{,}550$ pass the
science-target provenance gate. We show that a score cut alone does
not select astrophysical objects: above $S=3$, more than $99.5\%$ of raw
fibres in every score bin are sky or non-science fibres, and that fraction
rises with score up to $S=10$, above which the single remaining bin (337
fibres, one science target) is too sparse to constrain the trend. Applying the
provenance gate ($\OBJTYPE = \mathrm{TGT}$, $\texttt{COADD\_FIBERSTATUS} = 0$,
$\TARGETID > 0$) before any score cut, and a pre-declared threshold rule,
yields a released catalogue of $1{,}244$ objects --- the top
$5.7\times10^{-5}$ of the science-target population. We validate the catalogue
against a published contract of sixteen machine-checkable requirements
(eleven pass outright; two are reported quantities rather than thresholds;
one returns a weak positive signal; two fail by design, to force the release
to state its own defects), and report three results that constrain its
interpretation. First, the score is essentially uncorrelated with exposure
quality and source brightness (Spearman $|\rho_s| \le 0.10$ against five
independent measures), so the tail is not a faintness or short-exposure
artefact. Second, the score is not merely correlated with but almost entirely
\emph{explained by} the blue spectrograph arm's own reconstruction residual:
it is the dominant term of the score by construction ($94.9\%$ of released
objects have it as their worst-fit camera; the full three-camera regression
explains $R^2=0.78$ of the score's variance and collapses to $R^2=0.01$ when
it is dropped, while it alone, univariately, explains $R^2=0.20$), the
residual shows no
dependence on blue-arm signal-to-noise ($\rho_s=-0.01$), and its wavelength
distribution concentrates far more tightly in the observed frame than the
rest frame for spectroscopically confirmed objects --- evidence favouring a
fixed-wavelength calibration origin over a genuine spectral feature, though
we do not close the question here. Third, the catalogue's taxonomy of $23$
clusters and $9$ families, built over the $675$ objects with no SIMBAD or NED
counterpart, is structured but not separated in the model's own latent space
and must be read as a stratification of the candidate list rather than as
physical classes. A known-object recovery benchmark against five reference
classes recovers no class at its pre-declared bar; with reference samples of
$27$--$5{,}285$ objects the benchmark has little power (95\% upper limits up
to $12.5\%$ recovery for the rarest class) and excludes only a strong
recovery signal, so this work is reported as a validated data release and not
as a discovery. We name $30$ follow-up targets in five rules-based tiers and
run the cheapest of the named tests here: of four anomaly-selected
DESI-pipeline $z\ge4$ quasar candidates with no catalogued counterpart, the
released Legacy Survey photometry already refutes one on public data alone,
and we can neither confirm nor refute the remaining three, because the
published schema omits flux inverse variances and because one candidate's own
photometry sits inside a disclosed photometric-join defect --- among the
release defects we record openly rather than hide. We close with the open
tests, led by the absent selection function, that any population-level use of
this catalogue must clear first.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Wide-field spectroscopic surveys produce far more spectra than can be
inspected. Unsupervised outlier detection is the standard response
\cite{BaronPoznanski2017}, and it works: a model trained to reconstruct
typical spectra flags the ones it cannot. The approach has been carried into
DESI itself, both on the Bright Galaxy Survey \cite{Liang2023} and with
variational autoencoders on galaxy spectra \cite{Nicolaou2026}.

What such a search usually does not publish is the part that decides whether
its output means anything --- the exact model, the exact code, the exact
parent catalogue, the selection rule, the reason the rule was chosen, and the
tests the resulting list did \emph{not} pass. Without those, a candidate list
is unfalsifiable: a reader cannot tell a genuine spectral outlier from a
fibre placed on blank sky, and cannot reproduce either.

This paper publishes that part alongside the catalogue. Our contributions
are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{A sealed provenance chain} (Sec.~\ref{sec:provenance}): the
    SHA-256 of the parent zcatalog, of the archived model, of the inference
    code, and of every intermediate product, bound under one run contract and
    re-verified end to end at publication time.
  \item \textbf{A demonstration that the provenance gate, not the score cut,
    is what makes such a sample astrophysical} (Sec.~\ref{sec:gate}). Above
    $S=3$ the raw fibre population is more than $99.5\%$ sky or non-science in
    every score bin, and the contamination \emph{worsens} with score up to
    $S=10$, above which the single remaining bin is too sparse (337 fibres,
    one science target) to constrain the trend. This is a property of the
    substrate, not of our model, and it applies to any reconstruction-error
    search run on public coadds.
  \item \textbf{A published validation contract} of sixteen
    machine-checkable requirements (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}), with its
    checker and raw output released, including four release defects the
    release carries openly rather than hides.
  \item \textbf{Three measurements of what the score responds to}
    (Secs.~\ref{sec:quality}--\ref{sec:blue}, \ref{sec:latent}): it is not an exposure-quality
    proxy; it is almost entirely explained by the blue-arm reconstruction
    residual (full-model $R^2=0.78$, collapsing to $0.01$ without it;
    the blue arm alone, univariately, explains $R^2=0.20$), whose own
    wavelength distribution favours an instrumental over an astrophysical
    origin without closing the question; and the taxonomy it supports is
    descriptive rather than physical.
  \item \textbf{A negative, low-power recovery benchmark} (Sec.~\ref{sec:recovery}):
    against five published reference classes, no class is recovered at the
    pre-declared bar, and the benchmark's small reference samples give it
    little power to have found one (95\% upper limits up to $12.5\%$
    recovery for the rarest class).
  \item \textbf{A named, rules-based follow-up set}
    (Sec.~\ref{sec:followup}), with the cheapest test in the set --- a
    Lyman-break consistency check on public imaging --- executed here rather
    than deferred.
\end{enumerate}

The framing follows from the benchmark. A catalogue whose pre-declared
success condition is unmet is a data release with a validated selection, not
a discovery paper, and we report it as one throughout.
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and the provenance chain,
Sec.~\ref{sec:method} the model and the selection rule,
Sec.~\ref{sec:catalogue} the catalogue itself,
Sec.~\ref{sec:validation} the validation contract and what it establishes,
Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy} the descriptive taxonomy, Sec.~\ref{sec:followup} the
named follow-up set and the open tests, Sec.~\ref{sec:context} what this
catalogue does and does not bear on cosmologically,
Sec.~\ref{sec:repro} the reproduction path, and Sec.~\ref{sec:limits} what
this catalogue is not.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{DESI DR1}
We use Data Release 1 of DESI \cite{DESIDR1,DESIInstrument}: the public
\texttt{iron} spectroscopic reduction, its redshift catalogue
\texttt{zall-pix-iron.fits}, and the per-(survey, program, healpix) coadd
spectra, streamed and scored in place rather than bulk-downloaded. Redshifts,
spectral classifications and quality flags are the pipeline's own, fit by
Redrock \cite{Redrock} and documented in \cite{DESIPipeline,DESIBitmasks}.

The scan covered a $36{,}634$-group locator inventory, receipt-verified group
by group, and processed
$28{,}425{,}963$ raw rows.
% [DN].parent_raw_rows; locator_inventory.jsonl line count
Deduplication by last \TARGETID\ occurrence in lexical shard-then-row order
removed $878{,}740$ duplicate rows and left $27{,}547{,}223$ unique
\TARGETID s.
% [DN].n_duplicate_rows_removed, [DN].parent_unique_targetids
Of those, $21{,}793{,}550$ satisfy the science-target provenance gate defined
in Sec.~\ref{sec:gate};
% [DN].parent_science_unique_targetids ([STS] science_only.unique_targetids)
that number, not the full $27.5$ million, is the parent population of this
catalogue, and every selected fraction quoted below uses it. The
deduplication rule itself has no physical content: which of a repeated
\TARGETID's several DESI (survey, program) observations survives depends on
shard file names and row order, not on which exposure is better. We have not
quantified how many of the $1{,}244$ released objects sit near enough to
threshold that their alternate duplicate would score below it, and record
this as an open, unquantified caveat on near-threshold catalogue membership
rather than assert it is small.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The archived model and its calibration}
Spectra are scored by an archived autoencoder frozen for this run. The input
is a per-spectrum median-absolute-flux-normalised $496$-bin
resampling;
% [CAL].score_definition
the encoder compresses it to $128$ latent
dimensions,
% [DN].latent_n_dims (latent_000..latent_127 in the enriched table)
and the score is a standardised reconstruction-error statistic,
\begin{equation}
  S \;=\; \frac{\overline{\mathrm{MSE}} - \mu_{\rm MSE}}{\sigma_{\rm MSE}},
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
with $\overline{\mathrm{MSE}}$ the per-spectrum mean squared reconstruction
error and $(\mu_{\rm MSE}, \sigma_{\rm MSE}) = (0.8771, 1.3605)$ sealed before
the scan.
% [CAL].mse_mean, [CAL].mse_std, [CAL].anomaly_score_definition
A high $S$ therefore means a spectrum unlike the shapes the model learned to
reproduce; it is a distance in the model's own reconstruction metric and
carries no physical normalisation.

The model itself, \texttt{best\_model\_47k.pt}, predates the clean-rerun
contract this release is bound under and is treated here strictly as a
frozen, hash-sealed artifact (Sec.~\ref{sec:provenance}): its weights are
re-verified byte for byte, but its training corpus (parent survey, target
classes, selection, any quality cuts, and any overlap with the $21.8$
million science targets scanned here), its wavelength-to-bin mapping across
the three cameras, and its architecture beyond ``convolutional
autoencoder, $496\!\to\!128$'' are not documented in any artifact this
release controls. Two consequences follow directly from Sec.~\ref{sec:blue}:
if the training corpus is drawn from the same imaging/spectroscopic
population scored here, objects inside it would carry an artificially low
reconstruction error, a selection effect operating on the exact quantity
that defines the catalogue; and the per-camera bin count is a candidate
contributor, alongside the calibration explanation of Sec.~\ref{sec:blue},
to why the reconstruction loss is dominated by the $b$ camera. Neither can be
assessed without the missing documentation, which we did not reconstruct or
guess at for this release; closing it is a named prerequisite for any use of
this catalogue beyond the data-release-with-caveats framing adopted here.

The calibration constants were fit on a two-stage
probability-proportional-to-size cluster
sample
% [CAL].sampling_design = two-stage-pps-cluster/v2
of $200$ groups and $20{,}000$ held-out spectra, with a further $20{,}000$
reserved for a stability check.
% [CAL].n_groups_selected, n_fit, n_validation
The check requires
$|\mu^{\rm val}_{\rm MSE} - \mu_{\rm MSE}| \le 5\,\sigma_{\rm MSE}/\sqrt{n}$;
the observed deviation was $0.00543$ against a bound of
$0.0481$, and it passed.
% [CAL].stability_check.observed_deviation, .bound, .passed
The constants and the model weights were then frozen and hash-bound before any
spectrum of the survey was scored, so the score of a given spectrum is fixed
by the contract rather than by the sample it is scored with.

Across the full scan the score distribution is sharply peaked and
long-tailed: over all fibres the median is $-0.56$, the $99$th percentile is
$3.08$, and the maximum observed is
$24.42$.
% [DN].parent_all_quantiles, [SKY].max_score_observed
Over science targets alone the distribution is tighter --- median $-0.60$,
$99$th percentile $0.96$, maximum
$11.33$.
% [DN].parent_science_quantiles
The released threshold of $S>3$ therefore sits far above the science-target
$99$th percentile, and the released catalogue is the top
$5.7\times10^{-5}$ of the science-target
population.
% [DN].released_fraction_of_science_parent

\subsection{The science-target provenance gate}
\label{sec:gate}
A row enters the candidate pool only if
$\OBJTYPE = \mathrm{TGT}$ \emph{and} $\texttt{COADD\_FIBERSTATUS} = 0$
\emph{and} $\TARGETID > 0$, the last asserted rather than merely
filtered.
% [SKY].rule, [STS].science_target_rule
This gate is applied \emph{before} any score cut and is enforced by a
committed, re-runnable checker,
\artifactlink{pipelines/p1_highz_tracers/clean_rerun/gates/check_sample_provenance.py}{check\_sample\_provenance.py}
(full path in the reproducibility manifest).

The gate is not a formality, and the quantitative reason is the single most
transferable result in this paper. Figure~\ref{fig:skyfraction} and
Table~\ref{tab:skyfrac} give the fraction of raw fibres that are sky or
non-science as a function of score: it exceeds $99.5\%$ in every bin above
$S=3$ and \emph{increases} monotonically with score, from $99.53\%$ in
$[3,4)$ to $99.97\%$ in $[8,10)$; the sparsely populated top bin
($337$ fibres, one science target) sits at $99.70\%$.
% [SKY].bins; [DN].sky_fraction_min / _max
A naive top-score selection from this scan returns instrument artefacts. The
effect is a property of the public coadd substrate --- sky fibres are
reconstructed worst precisely because they contain no source --- so any
reconstruction-error search on DESI coadds that omits a provenance gate will
report the same population, whatever its model.

\subsection{The threshold rule}
The release threshold follows a rule fixed before the science-only counts
were inspected: from the grid $\{3,4,5,6,8,10\}$, take the largest threshold
whose science-only count is $\ge 300$; if that count exceeds $1{,}500$, step
to the next grid point unless doing so drops the count below
$300$.
% [THR].rule
The rule selects $S>3$ with $n=1{,}244$, reproduced independently from the
counts rather than read from the stored choice in check
V4.
% [THR].chosen_threshold, [VC] check V4

Fig.~\ref{fig:scoredist} plots both survival curves alongside the released
sample's score distribution. The two curves diverge by more than two orders
of magnitude, which is the same statement as Fig.~\ref{fig:skyfraction} read
cumulatively.

\subsection{Photometry and morphology}
Legacy Survey photometry is carried for every object. $441$ are typed
\texttt{PSF} (point-like), $316$ \texttt{REX}, $61$ \texttt{EXP}, $14$
\texttt{SER}, and $6$ \texttt{DEV}, with $406$ rows carrying an empty
\texttt{MORPHTYPE} --- a gap in the photometric join that the deposit must
close. The six values sum to the full released $1{,}244$.
% [DN].morphtype_counts
Only $6$ objects have a Gaia DR3 counterpart with a measured $G$ magnitude

\subsection{The score is not an exposure-quality proxy}
\label{sec:quality}
The first thing to rule out is that the score measures how badly a spectrum
was measured rather than what it contains. Within the science-target sample
the anomaly score is essentially uncorrelated with every available measure of
exposure quality: Spearman $\rho_s = +0.058$ against median coadd
signal-to-noise, $-0.076$ against coadd exposure time, $-0.093$ and $-0.096$
against $\texttt{TSNR2}_{\rm LRG}$ and $\texttt{TSNR2}_{\rm QSO}$, and
$-0.016$ against $r$-band flux (Fig.~\ref{fig:quality}).
% [VC] check V10
At $n=1{,}244$, four of these reach $p<0.05$; the three strongest are
negative (longer exposures, higher \texttt{TSNR2}, score marginally
\emph{lower}), while the coadd signal-to-noise coefficient is positive and
only marginal ($\rho_s=+0.058$, $p=0.039$). All five are negligible in size.

The claim this supports is narrow and negative: the $S>3$ tail is not
explained by faintness or short exposure. It is not evidence that the tail is
astrophysically interesting. V10 should be read as closing one explanation,
not as opening another.

\subsection{The score is driven by the blue arm}
\label{sec:blue}
DESI spectrographs record each fibre in three cameras, and the released
catalogue carries the reconstruction residual of each separately.
Decomposing by camera gives $\rho_s(S, r_B) = +0.527$ ($p \sim 10^{-89}$),
against $-0.037$ ($p = 0.19$) for $r_R$ and $-0.142$ ($p \sim 5\times10^{-7}$)
for $r_Z$.
% [DN].band_residual_spearman
The median residuals are correspondingly lopsided: $r_B = 2.53$ versus
$r_R = 1.03$ and $r_Z = 0.94$.
% [DN].band_residual_medians
Because $S$ is an exact affine function of \texttt{mean\_mse}
($\rho_s(S,\texttt{mean\_mse})=1.000$), $\rho_s(S,r_B)$ is a correlation
between the score and one of its own components, not an independent
measurement; a positive coefficient for whichever camera dominates the loss
follows arithmetically. The right statement is a variance decomposition:
regressing \texttt{mean\_mse} on $(r_B,r_R,r_Z)$ gives $R^2=0.777$; dropping
$r_B$ collapses it to $R^2=0.006$, while dropping $r_R$ or $r_Z$ leaves
$R^2=0.609$ or $0.201$. This is distinct from what $r_B$ explains in
isolation: the univariate regression of \texttt{mean\_mse} on $r_B$ alone
gives $R^2=0.198$ (against $0.001$ for $r_R$ and $0.004$ for $r_Z$). The two
readings are not interchangeable --- one is a drop-one variance
decomposition, the other a marginal fit --- and both are stated explicitly
wherever either is quoted, including in the abstract.
% [BAD].mean_mse_r2_univariate_rB/rR/rZ
The released \texttt{worst\_band} column makes the
same point directly: the $b$ camera is the worst-fit camera for $1{,}180$ of
the $1{,}244$ released objects ($94.9\%$; $60$ for $z$, $4$ for $r$), and the
released \texttt{peak\_residual\_wavelength} column concentrates hard against
the DESI blue cutoff (median $4057$\,\AA, minimum $3632$\,\AA, $89.5\%$ of
objects below $5930$\,\AA).
% [BAD].mean_mse_r2_full, .mean_mse_r2_drop_rB/rR/rZ, .worst_band_census
Figure~\ref{fig:bands} shows the three per-camera relations. The $b$-camera
panel is a near-monotonic locus; the other two are scatter.

Whatever the model responds to lives in the $b$ camera. Two readings survive
this measurement: blue-end sky-subtraction and calibration residuals, which
are a known difficulty at the blue limit of fibre spectrographs, or genuine
spectral behaviour at rest-frame wavelengths that land in the $b$ camera for
this redshift distribution. This is the single most consequential open
question about the catalogue, because the first reading would make much of
the list instrumental --- and unlike the released columns used above, it is
not fully undecided by the data already in hand. Two within-catalogue
diagnostics both lean instrumental without closing the question. First, the
$b$-camera residual has no detectable dependence on $b$-camera
signal-to-noise ($\rho_s(r_B,\texttt{snr}_b)=-0.008$, $p=0.78$; contrast
$\rho_s(r_R,\texttt{snr}_r)=-0.078$, $p=0.006$ and
$\rho_s(r_Z,\texttt{snr}_z)=-0.171$, $p\sim10^{-9}$), which disfavours the
simplest reading that the $b$ arm reconstructs worst because it is noisiest.
Second, for the $502$ objects with a secure pipeline redshift ($\ZWARN=0$),
\texttt{peak\_residual\_wavelength} is more tightly concentrated in the
\emph{observed} frame (median $4211$\,\AA, interquartile range $39\%$ of the
median) than in the rest frame after de-redshifting (median $2098$\,\AA,
interquartile range $68\%$ of the median): a genuine rest-frame spectral
feature should pile up at a fixed rest wavelength, and a fixed-wavelength
calibration artefact should pile up at a fixed observed wavelength, and the
data show the latter pattern.
% [BAD].residual_vs_snr_spearman, .restframe_test
These two diagnostics both favour the instrumental reading over the
astrophysical one, but neither is a closed test: they rule out one specific
noise-driven mechanism and one specific astrophysical mechanism, not every
member of either class. It is also cheap to test further on public data, and
we name the airmass/lunar-phase/sky-subtraction-residual version of that test
as OT-3 in Sec.~\ref{sec:opentests}. The diagnostics in this section are
computed by the committed script
\texttt{blue\_arm\_diagnostics\_2026-09-19.py}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:repro}), run on the released tables alone, with no
new scan and no network access.

\subsection{Known-object recovery}
\label{sec:recovery}
A candidate list can be tested against classes of genuinely unusual objects
that are already published: if the score is finding unusual astrophysics, it
should preferentially recover them. We cross-matched the released sample at
$1.5''$ against eleven candidate reference classes, of which five had usable
positions (four lacked identifiable coordinate columns, one had no known
catalogue identifier, and one timed out).
% [BEN].reference_manifest_summary, .parameters.radius_arcsec
The bar was declared in advance: at least one class recovered at
$>10\times$ enrichment with $\ge 5$ matches.

Table~\ref{tab:recovery} gives the outcome, with enrichment computed against
the $21{,}793{,}550$-object science-target parent (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}), the
same denominator used for every other selected fraction in this paper. One
broad-absorption-line quasar \cite{Gibson2009} matches, at $3.3\times$ the
base rate; a single match on a $N_{\rm ref}=5{,}285$ reference class, so this
enrichment carries a wide interval of its own and supports no standalone
claim. Roma-BZCAT blazars \cite{Massaro2015}, cataclysmic variables
\cite{RitterKolb2003}, Lyman-$\alpha$ emitters \cite{Ouchi2008} and
superluminous-supernova hosts \cite{Perley2016} return nothing.
% [BEN].results; [DN].benchmark.enrichment_science_denominator.bal_quasars
\emph{No reference class is recovered at the pre-declared bar}, and there is
no closed-loop candidate class.
% [DN].benchmark.closed_loop_candidates = 0

This should sharpen rather than soften that conclusion: the reference
footprints are small compared with the parent survey, so the test has little
power for rare classes. The $95\%$ upper limit on the
recovery of superluminous-supernova hosts is $12.5\%$, which excludes almost
nothing. The honest summary is that the benchmark rules out a strong
recovery signal and is consistent with anything weaker.

This is the result that makes the present work a data release with a
validated selection rather than a discovery paper, and we state it as such.

\section{Descriptive taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

\subsection{Construction}
The $675$ unmatched objects are grouped by PCA $\to$ UMAP \cite{UMAP} $\to$
HDBSCAN \cite{HDBSCAN} on four standardised features (zero mean, unit
variance): the sky position embedded on the unit sphere,
$(\cos\alpha\cos\delta,\sin\alpha\cos\delta,\sin\delta)$, together with the
anomaly score $S$, with $k$-nearest-neighbour noise reassignment, giving
$23$ clusters that roll up by shared (score-tier, dominant survey/programme)
identity into $9$ families (Table~\ref{tab:families},
Fig.~\ref{fig:families}); two families can share the same printed
(Tier, Survey/prog.) summary in Table~\ref{tab:families} while remaining
distinct, since the roll-up uses each cluster's own descriptor before
aggregation, not the family-level summary shown there. $n_{\rm PCA}=10$
components are requested but clipped to the $4$ available features, so PCA
performs a rotation only, not a dimensionality reduction.
% [FAM]; [VC] check V6
Family sizes sum to $675$ exactly, and the per-family counts agree with the
per-object labels (check V6). Every hyperparameter and the random seed
(\texttt{pca\_components=10, umap\_neighbors=15, umap\_min\_dist=0.05,
min\_cluster\_size=15, min\_samples=3, random\_state=42}) are stated here and
reused verbatim from the published run's own configuration file
(\texttt{flagship\_taxonomy\_v2\_manifest.json}), so this result is
reproducible from the released files and this manuscript alone.

An earlier construction of this taxonomy fed raw, unstandardised
$(S,\alpha,\delta)$ into the same pipeline. Right ascension is periodic at
$360^\circ$, so naive Euclidean distance on raw degrees splits objects near
$\alpha\approx0$ into maximally distant points; that earlier construction's
largest family had a naive RA span of $350.4^\circ$ --- the signature of
exactly this defect, not a genuinely sky-spanning family --- and its
hyperparameters and seed, while present in its own configuration file, were
never stated in that construction's own manuscript prose. A corrected
re-clustering, run on the identical $675$-object input with every
hyperparameter and the seed reused verbatim from that earlier run (isolating
the sky-embedding fix as the only changed variable; confirmed deterministic
under a bit-identical rerun), and compared against a (survey,
programme)-only baseline, produces the $23$ clusters/$9$ families reported
here: Adjusted Rand Index $0.60$ and Adjusted Mutual Information $0.63$
against the earlier, hash-bound partition
(\texttt{flagship\_taxonomy\_v2.json}, Table~\ref{tab:contract}) over the
same objects --- a moderate restructuring, not a dissolution: most of the
score-tier $\times$ survey/programme structure survives, but no earlier
family is reproduced unchanged and specific cluster boundaries are
provisional. Against the (survey, programme)-only baseline the corrected
families score ARI $0.26$/AMI $0.26$ --- weaker agreement, confirming the
score axis contributes real partition information beyond a bare survey/
programme relabelling, alongside the survey/programme structure quantified
directly below. Critically, fixing the wrap does
not make the largest family sky-compact: its true, wrap-aware RA span is
still $331.6^\circ$ (Table~\ref{tab:famsky}), so the
not-a-compact-sky-region conclusion below is not an artifact of the earlier
defect and is, if anything, reinforced by a correctly-measured span. Full
method, hyperparameters, and the per-family comparison table:

\subsection{The latent-space test}
\label{sec:latent}
A stratification built from the score and the sky is not, on its face, a
claim about spectra. The score $S$ is itself an affine function of the
$128$-dimensional latent reconstruction error, so the clustering did see a
scalar summary of that space; what it did not see is the space's
$128$-dimensional \emph{geometry}. We tested the partition against that
geometry directly: its silhouette over the $128$ latent dimensions is
$-0.0183$, against a $10{,}000$-draw label-permutation null of
$-0.0310 \pm 0.0063$ whose largest draw is $-0.0144$ (check V12,
Fig.~\ref{fig:silhouette}), recomputed against the corrected family labels
of Sec.~\ref{sec:taxonomy}.
% [DN].latent_silhouette, latent_null_mean, latent_null_sd, latent_null_max
The observed value exceeds every one of the $10{,}000$ permutation draws, so
the empirical $p = 0.0041$: the partition is distinguishable from a random
relabelling of the same score-derived quantity, though this test cannot by
itself separate structure attributable to $S$ from structure in the sky
coordinates.

But the absolute silhouette is \emph{negative}. A negative silhouette means
the typical object is, on average, closer to some other family's members than
to its own. The families are therefore not separated clusters in latent
space, and the correct reading is the weaker one: they are descriptive strata
of the candidate list that carry a small amount of latent-space information,
not physical classes. They are labelled with descriptors only, and no family
is given a physical class name. Re-clustering on the latents themselves is
the obvious next version (OT-2).

\subsection{Two families worth starting with}
Two families stand out for follow-up purposes. Family~6 ($31$ objects, a
single cluster) is the extreme-score family, with median $S = 4.68$, a
$95$th percentile of $6.52$, the largest median blue residual of any family
($r_B = 3.12$), and the highest-scoring unmatched object in the catalogue
($S = 7.15$).
% [FAM] family_id 6
Family~5 ($36$ objects) has the highest reliable-redshift fraction ($27/36$,
$75\%$) at a high median score of $3.62$ and no object above
$z = 2$: well-measured, low-redshift, high-score, and therefore the family in
which an instrumental or template explanation is most directly
testable.
% [FAM] family_id 5
Family~2, the same score tier's worst redshift reliability ($19/72$,
$26\%$), is not its mirror in every other respect: unlike the corresponding
comparison under the earlier construction, it carries $3$ pipeline
$\mathrm{SPECTYPE}=\mathrm{QSO}$ classifications (not necessarily at
$\ZWARN=0$) and $2$ AllWISE matches, so any follow-up there should
start from those existing identifications rather than from re-reduction
alone.

The families also inherit the limits of the cross-match. AllWISE coverage of
the unmatched subset is $6$ of $675$,
% [FAM] sum of n_wise over families
so mid-infrared colours cannot discriminate between families at all; the $74$
AllWISE matches in the full sample sit overwhelmingly in the matched half,
which is expected, since catalogued objects are catalogued because they are
detectable elsewhere.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]

\subsection{A Lyman-break check on the FT-A candidates}
\label{sec:dropout}
The cheapest test in the whole follow-up set costs no observing time. A
quasar at redshift $z$ places Lyman-$\alpha$ at $1216\,(1+z)$~\AA, and the
% derivation: lambda_obs = lambda_rest (1+z) with lambda_Lya = 1215.67 A;
% computed per object by make_draft_figures.py -> [DN].ft_a.lya_observed_angstrom.
% Band edges quoted below are the approximate DECam g/r effective ranges of the
% Legacy Surveys (Ref. LegacySurveys) and are used only as a qualitative

intergalactic medium absorbs essentially everything blueward of it, so any
broadband filter lying entirely below the break must go dark. For the four
FT-A candidates the break falls at $6484$, $7530$, $8587$ and $8616$~\AA,
so the Legacy Survey $g$ band ($\approx 4000$--$5500$~\AA) is fully blueward
of the break for all four, and the $r$ band ($\approx 5500$--$7000$~\AA) for
the three at $z > 4.5$.

both halves for every candidate it applies to: a dropout in every band fully

straddles or sits just redward of it. We apply that test uniformly below,
which changes one of this section's conclusions relative to the released
$g$-only check.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item[Refuted] \TARGETID~$39628216868014338$ ($z = 5.194$) carries
    $f_g = 4.33\nmgy$ ($94.7$th percentile of the whole catalogue; $66$
    objects, including itself, carry a $g$ flux at least this bright), with
    $f_g/f_r = 59$.

    $4.33\nmgy$ $g$-band flux ($m = 22.5 - 2.5\log_{10}(4.33) \approx 20.9$
    AB mag, orders of magnitude
    above any plausible Legacy Survey noise floor) is inconsistent with the
    pipeline redshift on the physics alone, independent of any percentile
    ranking within this catalogue. Its $\Delta\chi^2$ of $22.0$ is also the
    weakest of the four, and its morphology is \texttt{SER} rather than
    point-like. We classify it as \emph{disfavoured by public imaging} ---
    either the photometry is blended or the redshift solution is wrong ---
    and retain it in the table so that the tier rule can be audited.
  \item[Undecidable from the release] The other three candidates cannot be
    settled by the released photometry, for two distinct reasons.
    \TARGETID~$39627849358909321$
    ($z=4.334$; $\Delta\chi^2=4205$, by far the strongest redshift fit in the
    tier) carries $f_g=-0.019\nmgy$ (consistent with zero, as the $g$-band
    half of the test requires) but also $\texttt{morphtype}=$ empty and
    $f_{W1}=f_{W2}=\texttt{sersic}=\texttt{shape\_r}=0$ exactly --- the exact
    signature of the $406$-row photometric-join gap disclosed in
    Sec.~\ref{sec:catalogue}, not a clean measurement. Its own $r$- and
    $z$-band fluxes ($f_r=0.886\nmgy$, $f_z=0.286\nmgy$, $f_r/f_z=3.1$) are
    backwards for the redshift they would need to support: at $z=4.334$ the
    $r$ band is $\sim\!66\%$ forest-absorbed while $z$ is unabsorbed
    continuum on a red-rising spectrum, so the expected ordering is
    $f_r\lesssim f_z$, not $f_r\approx 3f_z$. We do not adopt either reading;
    the object needs the same forced photometry as the other two before it
    can be classified either way.
% [DN].ft_a[3]
    The two $z \approx 6.07$ candidates carry $f_g = 0.283$ and
    $0.296\nmgy$ --- $0.98$ and $1.02$ times the catalogue median $g$ flux ---
    values consistent with either a faint detection or a non-detection.
% [DN].ft_a[0], ft_a[1]
    Neither has a photometric-join flag, but both also carry $f_r=0.255$ and
    $0.330\nmgy$, comparable to or larger than their own $g$ fluxes in a band

    Because the released schema omits flux inverse variances for all three
    objects, none of these values can be distinguished from noise, so none of
    the three can be confirmed or refuted here. Legacy Survey DR10 forced
    photometry with inverse variances, applied to all four bands for all
    three objects, settles this at zero observing cost and is the immediate
    next step; until then this tier's verdict is \emph{one refuted, three
    undecided}, not \emph{one refuted, one supported, two undecided}.

We emphasise what this does and does not establish. One candidate has been
removed by public data alone, and none has cleared the full break test on
the photometry released here; this makes no object in this catalogue a
discovery, and a $\ZWARN = 0$ Redrock solution on a low-signal-to-noise
spectrum fails routinely at high redshift. The remaining tests, in cost
order, are: the DR10 forced-photometry query above; visual inspection of the
public DR1 coadd for a Lyman-$\alpha$ break and forest; DESI DR2 repeat
coadds where the tile is revisited; and only then confirmation spectroscopy,
optical for the $z \approx 4$ candidate and near-infrared for the $z \approx
6$ pair.

\subsection{Open tests}
\label{sec:opentests}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item[OT-1 Selection function] No injection-recovery experiment has been
    run for this model on this substrate. The catalogue therefore has no
    measured completeness or purity, and no population-level inference may be
    drawn from it. This is the gate any rate, abundance, or cosmological use
    must clear first, and it requires injecting known spectral classes into
    DR1 coadds at controlled signal-to-noise and re-scoring with the
    hash-bound model --- GPU-scale work, not local, and genuinely not run for
    this release. The experiment is fully specified (inject each of the five
    reference classes' template spectra from Table~\ref{tab:recovery} at a
    grid of signal-to-noise ratios into real DR1 sky/noise realizations,
    re-score with \texttt{best\_model\_47k.pt} under the same run contract,
    and report per-class recovery vs.\ injected signal-to-noise) but not
    executed; we state the specification rather than a placeholder so a
    reader can judge the remaining work.
  \item[OT-2 Latent-space taxonomy] Re-clustering on the $128$ latent
    dimensions rather than on $(S, \alpha, \delta)$, following the negative
    silhouette of Sec.~\ref{sec:latent}. Cheap; the released catalogue
    already carries the latents.
  \item[OT-3 Blue-arm diagnosis] Compare the $b$-camera residual against
    airmass, lunar phase, and blue-end sky-subtraction residuals in the DR1
    exposure tables, to separate calibration from astrophysics. This decides
    how much of the catalogue is astrophysical and is the highest-value open
    test after OT-1.
  \item[OT-4 Wider recovery benchmark] Six of eleven candidate reference
    classes lacked usable positions and were not tested; recovering their
    coordinates would materially strengthen or weaken
    Sec.~\ref{sec:recovery}.
  \item[OT-5 Deeper scan] A self-supervised model trained on the full
    science-target spectrum population, rather than the archived autoencoder
    (whose training-set size and composition this release does not
    document, Sec.~\ref{sec:method}), is the realistic route to a confirmed
    anomalous class.
\end{description}

\section{What this catalogue bears on}
\label{sec:context}

This catalogue was built inside a programme whose interest in DESI spectra is
an early-universe one: assembling anomalies in existing public data that
strain single-field inflation, and attaching an explicit discriminator to
each. It is worth being precise about how little of that this release
supports, because the temptation to over-connect a candidate list to a
cosmological question is exactly what a provenance-first release exists to
resist.

Three statements are defensible. First, the catalogue is
\emph{infrastructure} for such a programme rather than evidence within it: it
supplies a reproducible, hash-bound path from a public survey to a candidate
list, and a validation contract a third party can re-run. Second, the only
channel by which this particular catalogue could feed an early-universe
abundance argument is its high-redshift end --- the $36$ reliable-redshift
objects at $z \ge 4$ and $16$ at $z \ge 6$, of which the four unmatched FT-A
candidates are the interesting subset. Third, that channel is closed at
present, and for two independent reasons: the candidates are unconfirmed, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:dropout} has already removed one of four on public data alone;
and without OT-1 the catalogue has no selection function, so it admits no
abundance, rate, or number-density statement of any kind.

We also record a null from the same programme that bounds what a catalogue
like this could ever contribute. Local-shape primordial non-Gaussianity,
parameterised by the amplitude $f_{\rm NL}$ of the bispectrum
$B_\Phi = 2f_{\rm NL}[P(k_1)P(k_2)+2\ \mathrm{perms}]$, shifts the abundance
of rare, high-redshift haloes through the tail of the mass function. For the
amplitude this programme's matter-dominated-contraction bounce predicts
($f_{\rm NL}=-35/16=-2.1875$), applying the LoVerde, Miller, Shandera \&
Verde (2008) \cite{LoVerde2008} non-Gaussian correction to a Planck-2018
\cite{Planck2018} $\Lambda$CDM mass function (Eisenstein \& Hu 1998
\cite{EisensteinHu1998} transfer function) shifts the $z\approx
10$--$14$, $\log M_h/M_\odot\approx11.5$ halo abundance by $-6\%$ to $-16\%$
relative to the Gaussian case --- a suppression, not an enhancement, so it
cannot explain the reported early-galaxy over-abundance
\cite{Labbe2023,BoylanKolchin2023}. That shift is a factor of roughly $1.06$
to $1.19$, well inside the $\gtrsim0.3$~dex ($\approx2\times$) stellar-mass
systematic floor of current high-redshift photometric censuses, not more
than an order of magnitude below it; a factor-of-two abundance effect at
this halo mass and redshift would require $|f_{\rm NL}|\sim30$, far outside
current CMB and large-scale-structure constraints. The full computation is
the committed, deterministic script
\artifactlink{research/anomaly_map/ledger6_png_highz_abundance.py}{ledger6\_png\_highz\_abundance.py}
(no fitting, no randomness; SHA-256 \texttt{f1121e2b79c4\ldots}), whose output
is \artifactlink{research/anomaly_map/outputs/ledger6_png_highz_abundance.json}{ledger6\_png\_highz\_abundance.json}.
% research/anomaly_map/LEDGER6_DISCRIMINATOR_BRIEF_2026-09-02.md
The high-redshift abundance is therefore not where that class of model is
measurable, whatever a candidate catalogue contributes to it. Publishing that
null beside the catalogue is the point: it is what keeps the release from
being read as evidence it is not.

\section{What this catalogue is not}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item It is not a discovery catalogue. No object here is reported as a
    confirmed unusual source; selection-pipeline output alone never promotes
    an object to notable.
  \item It is not a statistically characterised sample. Without OT-1 there is
    no completeness, no purity, and no selection function, so no population
    inference is available.
  \item Its families are not physical classes (Sec.~\ref{sec:latent}).
  \item Its unmatched fraction is not a novelty fraction: it includes objects
    simply outside other surveys' footprints or below their detection limits,
    and the population is faint enough that only six objects in the whole
    catalogue have a Gaia counterpart.
  \item Its anomaly score is a distance from one model's learned
    reconstruction, not a physically motivated significance, and
    Sec.~\ref{sec:blue} shows it is dominated by one spectrograph arm.
  \item Its high-redshift candidates are pipeline redshifts, not
    confirmations --- and one of four has already failed a public-data check.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We publish $1{,}244$ DESI DR1 science-target spectra selected by
reconstruction-error anomaly score --- the top $5.7\times10^{-5}$ of the
$21.8$ million science targets scanned --- with a sealed provenance chain, a
published validation contract of sixteen machine-checkable requirements, a
descriptive taxonomy of the unmatched subset, and a named follow-up set.

Four results are worth carrying forward independently of the catalogue. A
score cut without a provenance gate selects sky fibres, not objects, and the
contamination worsens with score up to $S=10$, above which the trend is
unconstrained by a single sparse bin; this is a property of the public coadd
substrate and applies to any reconstruction-error search run on it. Within
the science-target sample the score is not a proxy for exposure quality or
brightness. The score is almost entirely explained by the blue spectrograph
arm's own reconstruction residual (full three-camera model $R^2=0.78$,
collapsing to $0.01$ when the blue arm is dropped; the blue arm alone,
univariately, explains $R^2=0.20$); that residual shows no dependence on
blue-arm signal-to-noise and concentrates more tightly in the observed
wavelength frame than the rest frame, evidence that leans instrumental
without closing the question, and we name the decisive external test as
OT-3. And the taxonomy's families, tested in the model's own latent
space, are structured but not separated --- descriptive strata, not classes.

The known-object recovery benchmark does not clear its pre-declared bar, but
with reference samples as small as $27$ objects it also has little power to
have found a strong recovery signal (95\% upper limit up to $12.5\%$ for the
rarest class); the one follow-up test cheap enough to run here refutes one of
four high-redshift candidates on public imaging alone and cannot decide the
other three. We therefore report the work as what it is: reproducible data
infrastructure with a validated selection, a stratified candidate list, and a
named list of the tests it has not yet passed.
