## 1. The Equation to Solve

$$
\ddot{\zeta}^{(2)} + \frac{2}{t}\dot{\zeta}^{(2)} = -\frac{3\,C^2}{t^4}
$$

where C = C(x) is the spatially-varying amplitude of the first-order growing mode.

Same as the first-order equation:

$$
\zeta_h^{(2)} = D_1 + \frac{D_2}{t}

The D_1 constant mode and D_2/t growing mode are the same two modes as before.

## 3. Particular Solution

We need a particular solution to ddot{zeta}_p + (2/t) dot{zeta}_p = -3C^2/t^4.

### Method: Variation of parameters.

The homogeneous solutions are y_1 = 1 and y_2 = 1/t.

The Wronskian:

$$
W = y_1 y_2' - y_2 y_1' = (1)(-1/t^2) - (1/t)(0) = -\frac{1}{t^2}
$$

The particular solution is:

$$
\zeta_p^{(2)} = -y_1\int\frac{y_2\,S}{W}\,dt + y_2\int\frac{y_1\,S}{W}\,dt
$$

where S(t) = -3C^2/t^4.

### Computing the integrals:

**First integral:**

$$
\int\frac{y_2\,S}{W}\,dt = \int\frac{(1/t)(-3C^2/t^4)}{-1/t^2}\,dt = \int\frac{-3C^2}{t^5}\cdot(-t^2)\,dt = \int\frac{3C^2}{t^3}\,dt
$$

$$
= 3C^2\int t^{-3}\,dt = 3C^2\cdot\frac{t^{-2}}{-2} = -\frac{3C^2}{2t^2}
$$

**Second integral:**

$$
\int\frac{y_1\,S}{W}\,dt = \int\frac{(1)(-3C^2/t^4)}{-1/t^2}\,dt = \int\frac{-3C^2}{t^4}\cdot(-t^2)\,dt = \int\frac{3C^2}{t^2}\,dt
$$

$$
= 3C^2\int t^{-2}\,dt = 3C^2\cdot\frac{t^{-1}}{-1} = -\frac{3C^2}{t}
$$

### Assembling the particular solution:

$$
\zeta_p^{(2)} = -(1)\left(-\frac{3C^2}{2t^2}\right) + \frac{1}{t}\left(-\frac{3C^2}{t}\right)
$$

$$
= \frac{3C^2}{2t^2} - \frac{3C^2}{t^2}
$$

$$
\boxed{\zeta_p^{(2)} = -\frac{3C^2}{2t^2}}
$$

### Verification:

$$
\dot{\zeta}_p^{(2)} = -\frac{3C^2}{2}\cdot(-2t^{-3}) = \frac{3C^2}{t^3}
$$

$$
\ddot{\zeta}_p^{(2)} = 3C^2\cdot(-3t^{-4}) = -\frac{9C^2}{t^4}
$$

Check: ddot{zeta}_p + (2/t) dot{zeta}_p = -9C^2/t^4 + (2/t)(3C^2/t^3) = -9C^2/t^4 + 6C^2/t^4 = -3C^2/t^4.

**CONFIRMED.** The particular solution satisfies the equation exactly.

## 4. General Second-Order Solution

$$
\zeta^{(2)} = D_1 + \frac{D_2}{t} - \frac{3C^2}{2t^2}
$$

### Selecting the physical mode:

The constants D_1 and D_2 are determined by initial conditions. Since we are interested in the second-order solution GENERATED by the first-order growing mode (starting from zero initial second-order perturbation in the far past), we set D_1 = D_2 = 0 at early times.

**Justification:** At early times |t| -> infinity, the source -3C^2/t^4 is negligible, and the particular solution -3C^2/(2t^2) vanishes. The homogeneous piece D_1 + D_2/t is the "initial" second-order perturbation. If we start with only first-order perturbations (no initial second-order perturbations), then D_1 = D_2 = 0.

More carefully: we should match to the Bunch-Davies vacuum at early times, which means all non-Gaussianity is GENERATED by the nonlinear evolution (not present in the initial state). This requires the second-order perturbation to vanish as t -> -infinity, which is satisfied by D_1 = D_2 = 0.

Wait -- D_1 = 0 is needed because D_1 is constant and would survive at early times. D_2/t vanishes as |t| -> infinity. So actually only D_1 = 0 is required by the initial condition. D_2 can be nonzero.

However, the D_2/t mode at second order corresponds to a second-order growing mode that is DEGENERATE with the particular solution (both grow as t -> 0). The physical content is:

- D_2/t is a "free" second-order growing mode (not sourced by first order)
- -3C^2/(2t^2) is the SOURCED second-order growing mode

For Bunch-Davies initial conditions with no initial non-Gaussianity: D_2 = 0.

**The physical second-order solution is:**

$$
\boxed{\zeta^{(2)}(t,\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{3\,C(\mathbf{x})^2}{2\,t^2}}
$$

## 6. Interpreting This Result

### What does f_NL = -5/4 mean?

This is the f_NL from the ZEROTH-ORDER gradient expansion -- i.e., from the purely local (homogeneous) nonlinear evolution of zeta, governed by:

$$
\ddot{\zeta} + \frac{2}{t}\dot{\zeta} + \frac{3}{2}\dot{\zeta}^2 = 0
$$

**This captures the nonlinear coupling of the growing mode to itself through the local Raychaudhuri equation.**

### How does this compare to the in-in result?

- Cai et al. (2009): f_NL = -35/8 = -4.375
- Li & Brandenberger (2016): f_NL ~ -2.19
- **Gradient expansion (zeroth order): f_NL = -5/4 = -1.25**

The gradient expansion gives a SMALLER magnitude than either in-in result. This tells us something important.

### Why the discrepancy?

The gradient expansion at zeroth order captures ONLY the nonlinear evolution of the growing mode on superhorizon scales. But the in-in calculation also captures the **nonlinear mode-mode coupling at horizon crossing.**

In the in-in approach, the bispectrum receives contributions from:

1. **Superhorizon evolution (local growth):** The growing mode evolves nonlinearly on superhorizon scales, generating zeta^(2) ~ (zeta^(1))^2. This is what the gradient expansion computes.

2. **Horizon-crossing coupling:** When modes cross the Hubble horizon during contraction, the nonlinear interactions generate additional contributions to the bispectrum. These involve the OSCILLATORY parts of the mode functions and cannot be captured by the long-wavelength approximation.

**The gradient expansion result f_NL = -5/4 is the superhorizon-evolution contribution only.** The full f_NL is the SUM of the superhorizon contribution and the horizon-crossing contribution.

### Connection to the field redefinition:

In the in-in formalism, the "field redefinition" contribution to f_NL is:

$$
f_{\rm NL}^{\rm FR} = +\frac{5}{4}
$$

This comes from the nonlinear relation between the field perturbation delta-phi and the curvature perturbation zeta at second order (the "gauge transformation" piece in Maldacena 2003).

**Remarkably, the gradient expansion gives f_NL = -5/4, which is exactly MINUS the field-redefinition contribution.**

This is NOT a coincidence. The gradient expansion in the uniform-field gauge captures the nonlinear evolution of zeta directly. In the in-in formalism, the same physics is split between:
- The field-redefinition piece (+5/4): the nonlinear gauge transformation between delta-phi and zeta
- The intrinsic piece (f_NL^intrinsic): the cubic-action contribution from the in-in integral

The gradient expansion gives the NET LOCAL contribution, which is the sum of what the in-in approach calls "field redefinition" plus the "late-time (superhorizon) part of the intrinsic integral."

Since the field redefinition gives +5/4 and the gradient expansion gives -5/4 for the total local part, the late-time superhorizon part of the intrinsic integral must be -5/2:

gradient_expansion = FR + SH_intrinsic

-5/4 = +5/4 + SH_intrinsic

SH_intrinsic = -5/2

And the total in-in result is:

f_NL^total = FR + intrinsic = FR + SH_intrinsic + HC_intrinsic = +5/4 + (-5/2) + HC_intrinsic

So: HC_intrinsic = f_NL^total - 5/4 + 5/2 = f_NL^total + 5/4

For Cai et al. (f_NL = -35/8): HC_intrinsic = -35/8 + 5/4 = -35/8 + 10/8 = -25/8

For Li & Brandenberger (f_NL ~ -35/16): HC_intrinsic = -35/16 + 5/4 = -35/16 + 20/16 = -15/16

## 7. Careful Decomposition: What the Gradient Expansion Captures

### The gradient expansion operates in the uniform-field gauge:

delta-phi = 0, and all perturbations are in zeta (the spatial metric perturbation).

The nonlinear equation ddot{zeta} + (2/t)dot{zeta} + (3/2)dot{zeta}^2 = 0 is the EXACT evolution for the local expansion rate in the uniform-field gauge, at zeroth order in spatial gradients.

### The in-in formalism operates with the Maldacena action:

The third-order action S_3[zeta] is derived by expanding the Einstein-Hilbert action + scalar field action to third order in zeta, IN the comoving gauge (which IS the uniform-field gauge). The field redefinition is a transformation from the "interaction picture zeta" to the "Heisenberg picture zeta" at the boundary.

### The key distinction:

The gradient expansion computes zeta^(2) as a function of zeta^(1) by solving the nonlinear superhorizon evolution equation. This gives the COMPLETE local-type non-Gaussianity on superhorizon scales.

The in-in formalism computes the bispectrum (a three-point correlator) by integrating the cubic action over ALL TIME, including the subhorizon regime. The bispectrum receives contributions from:
- **Subhorizon interactions** (mode-mode coupling during horizon crossing)
- **Superhorizon evolution** (nonlinear growth after horizon crossing)
- **Field redefinition** (nonlinear gauge transformation at the evaluation time)

The gradient expansion includes the superhorizon evolution and the field redefinition (both are captured by the nonlinear ODE in the uniform-field gauge), but MISSES the subhorizon (horizon-crossing) contributions.

### Therefore:

$$
f_{\rm NL}^{\rm total} = f_{\rm NL}^{\rm gradient\;expansion} + f_{\rm NL}^{\rm horizon\;crossing}
$$

$$
f_{\rm NL}^{\rm total} = -\frac{5}{4} + f_{\rm NL}^{\rm HC}
$$

For Cai et al.: f_NL^HC = -35/8 + 5/4 = -35/8 + 10/8 = **-25/8 = -3.125**

For Li & Brandenberger: f_NL^HC = -35/16 + 5/4 = -35/16 + 20/16 = **-15/16 = -0.9375**
