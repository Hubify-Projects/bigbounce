Major concerns
MAJOR — The 268,519-object “validated catalog-grade” headline is not a statistically coherent catalog.
Sections II.D and III, Table I, and the three-tier definition combine four surveys selected under fundamentally different rules:
DESI: S>5S>5S>5;
SDSS: a fixed-size 77,905-object “continuity slice” chosen specifically to reproduce the old cross-transfer count;
Planck: a fixed top-200 count;
NEOWISE: a fixed top-1% count followed by a geometric mask.
These are not comparable detection criteria, nor do they define a common false-positive rate, completeness level, likelihood threshold, or operating point. Most seriously, Section III.C explicitly states that the SDSS count of 77,905 is “not a physically or statistically motivated anomaly threshold,” while the native S>5S>5S>5 cut would select only 12 objects and the native top-1% cut would select 19,253. Nevertheless, all 77,905 are included in the validated headline.
This makes the headline count partly a bookkeeping construction rather than the output of a defined anomaly detector. The fact that the deduplication script reproduces the arithmetic 274,353→268,519274{,}353\rightarrow268{,}519274,353→268,519 does not validate the scientific membership of the input catalogs.
Required correction: define, before examining the final counts, a defensible survey-specific operating point tied to measurable quantities such as recovery completeness, contamination, held-out score distributions, or false-discovery control. The arbitrary SDSS continuity slice must not be included in a catalog described as “validated.” At minimum, headline counts should be recomputed using the native SDSS top-1% or another independently justified threshold, with completeness and contamination characterized.
MAJOR — NEOWISE does not satisfy the manuscript’s own definition of detector validation.
Sections II.D, III.H, VI.D(ii), Table I, and Fig. 10 acknowledge that the NEOWISE test “passes by construction”: synthetic positions are placed outside the adopted ecliptic mask and are “recovered” by applying that same mask. This tests code implementation, not anomaly detection.
Yet the 419 NEOWISE entries are included in the “validated catalog-grade” 268,519-object subset. Calling the result “geometry-QA” rather than “detector-sensitivity PASS” is transparent, but it does not solve the logical problem: a catalog cannot be classified as validated by replacing the relevant validation test with a deterministic mask check.
Required correction: either perform a genuine feature-space injection/recovery test on the NEOWISE model, including realistic photometric uncertainties and survey-cadence systematics, or move NEOWISE out of the validated tier. The term “validated catalog-grade” must not cover objects for which detector sensitivity has never been measured.
MAJOR — The DESI headline is dominated by non-primary science spectra, and the current tests do not establish that this population is astrophysical rather than instrumental or operational.
Section III.A finds that only 2,468 of 190,015 deduplicated DESI anomaly clusters match primary science-class targets at 1′′1''1′′, implying that approximately 98.7% of the DESI headline population consists of sky fibers, filler spectra, secondary/ToO programs, calibration-related observations, or other non-primary classes. This is not a minor scope issue: DESI contributes 195,829 of the 274,353 pre-dedup detections entering the validated headline.
The manuscript subsequently argues that the sample is astrophysical because nearly all anomaly records join to a Redrock catalog and receive a spectral class. A database join or Redrock classification is not sufficient to exclude instrumental contamination, especially for spectra outside the primary science-target selection. The top-200 visual inspection tests only about 0.1%0.1\%0.1% of the DESI catalog and is heavily rank-conditioned. It cannot support a global artifact bound of 1.5% for the full 195,829-object population. Moreover, the manuscript itself identifies a large B-dominant component—44,436 objects—for which calibration error remains an open hypothesis in Section VI.C.
Required correction: partition DESI by target-bit class, observing program, fiber type, exposure/coadd status, tile/night, camera arm, and known calibration flags. Estimate contamination and recovery separately for primary science targets, sky fibers, standards, secondary targets, and filler observations. The 195,829 full-stream detections should not be called a catalog of astrophysical anomaly candidates until these classes are individually validated. A scientifically cleaner primary result would be the science-target subset.
MAJOR — The injection/recovery tests do not establish catalog completeness or reliability at the adopted selection thresholds.
Sections II.D and VI.D(ii) use a heuristic requirement of at least 50% recovery for a nominal 5σ5\sigma5σ injected signal. This does not connect directly to the actual anomaly-score thresholds used to construct most tiers. The signal families are also highly restricted:
broad continuum/emission perturbations for DESI;
continuum dips for SDSS and LAMOST;
Gaussian bumps for Planck;
no detector injection for NEOWISE.
Recovery of a chosen synthetic morphology does not validate a heterogeneous anomaly catalog. Indeed, the manuscript demonstrates extreme morphology dependence: SDSS recovers 64% for continuum dips but only 7.2% for emission-line injections, while DESI single-pixel lines require at least 15σ15\sigma15σ.
The phrase “validation establishes that the 268,519 subset is real” in the abstract is therefore unsupported. These tests show sensitivity to selected artificial perturbations, not that individual catalog entries are real astrophysical anomalies or that the catalog has a controlled contamination rate.
Required correction: distinguish clearly among score stability, injected-signal sensitivity, astrophysical purity, and catalog completeness. Provide recovery surfaces over physically motivated anomaly families, amplitude, width, S/N, redshift and observing conditions. MAJOR — The Planck validation and statistical significance are compromised by training/scoring overlap and spatial dependence.
Section III.F states that the full 200,000-patch bank, including training patches, is scored and that the released top-200 is not held out. The manuscript then treats the presence of 48 top-200 patches in the 15% validation split, versus 30 expected, as evidence against memorization, with an exact binomial p=5.5×10−4p=5.5\times10^{-4}p=5.5×10−4.
This calculation assumes independent Bernoulli assignments. The manuscript itself acknowledges that adjacent 10∘×10∘10^\circ\times10^\circ10∘×10∘ patches overlap or share sky structure, so the independence assumption is false. The claim that correlation “inflates the effective sample size” is also backwards: positive spatial correlation normally reduces the effective sample size and makes the nominal binomial ppp-value too small. The paper later describes the quoted value as a lower bound on the true tail probability, but that does not rescue its use as quantitative evidence.
More fundamentally, membership enrichment in the validation split is not a substitute for scoring a fully independent sky-patch set with a frozen model. The lack of the checkpoint and patch tensor in the release further limits reproducibility.
Required correction: train on spatially separated sky blocks and evaluate anomaly rankings on disjoint HEALPix regions, with block-bootstrap or rotation-based uncertainty. Recompute the top-patch catalog on held-out regions. The current binomial significance should be removed.
MAJOR — The “17.8% genuine novelty fraction” does not measure genuine novelty.
Section IV.A defines 178 of the top 1,000 DESI anomalies as “genuinely novel” because no counterpart was found in 18 selected catalogs. Absence from those catalogs does not establish physical novelty. It may result from:
positional uncertainty or an inappropriate fixed radius;
footprint or depth differences;
catalog incompleteness;
source deblending;
spectra of sky/filler fibers;
missing object-level identifiers;
failed or low-quality targeting;
moving/transient sources;
ordinary sources below catalog thresholds.
This is particularly problematic because the DESI spectra themselves necessarily arise from a targeting or fiber-assignment process. The manuscript does not present manual validation of all 178, forced-photometry tests, image-level non-detection limits, local-density-dependent match probabilities, or a false-nonmatch estimate.
The paper correctly notes that this is a top-1,000 point estimate and not survey-wide, but the terminology “genuine novelty fraction” remains unjustified.
Required correction: rename the quantity as an “unmatched fraction against the selected 18-catalog baseline” unless object-by-object validation establishes otherwise. A credible novelty estimate requires probabilistic cross-matching, local density and astrometric uncertainties, image inspection, forced photometry, and classification of the unmatched cases.

MAJOR — Reproducibility is asserted, but several load-bearing products were lost, unavailable, or reconstructed from incomplete provenance.
The manuscript reports:

the Planck checkpoint and patch tensor unavailable;
NEOWISE feature tables available only pod-side;
an unrecoverable eROSITA production score axis;
a synthetic Gaia fallback that entered earlier catalog versions;

Required correction: separate “count reproducibility,” “membership reproducibility,” and “end-to-end pipeline reproducibility.” Public release and independent rerun of all load-bearing checkpoints, raw-to-feature preprocessing, random seeds and score tables should be completed before publication. The paper repeatedly acknowledges that:

the SDSS count is an arbitrary continuity slice;
LAMOST is a failed exploratory tier;
Planck and NEOWISE counts are predetermined top-NNN selections;

Under these conditions, the total of 268,519 is dominated by choices of stream composition and fixed selection fraction, rather than a demonstrated increase in scientifically usable anomaly discovery. Calling it a “process-volume figure” is honest, but process volume is not itself a strong scientific result for PRD.
Required correction: center the paper on a well-defined, science-target catalog with uniform validation, quantified selection functions, and concrete astrophysical discoveries. The engineering scan volume can remain a secondary result.
Minor concerns
MINOR — The abstract is excessively long and contains conclusions that require multiple qualifications.
The abstract functions as a compressed paper, with detailed count chains, scripts, gate taxonomy, fNLf_{\rm NL}fNL​, NANOGrav and provenance discussion. It is difficult to identify the scientific result. It should be shortened substantially and should not state that validation establishes the subset is “real.”
MINOR — The distinction between native and cross-transfer results remains confusing.
Figures 2–4 and Table IV prominently show historical cross-transfer outputs that are explicitly not the released science product. Although captions disclose this, readers can easily mistake these populations for the final catalog. Cross-transfer diagnostics should be moved to an appendix or visually marked as failed/historical.
MINOR — Section VI.B is inconsistent with the adopted Path-C framing.
It says the transfer-learning approach “used for SDSS” deliberately exploits model dependence, whereas Section III.C states that the released SDSS tier is a native re-score and that the transfer result is a failed baseline. The section should be rewritten to distinguish the historical diagnostic from the final catalog.
MINOR — The interpretation of the SDSS score–redshift correlation is overstated.
Section III.C states that instrumental effects “cannot” produce a score increasing with redshift. Redshift correlates with S/N, wavelength coverage, target selection, spectral features, pipeline fit quality and observing conditions. A very small ρ=0.036\rho=0.036ρ=0.036 does not uniquely identify astrophysical rarity. The language should be weakened and multivariate controls added.
MINOR — The DESI science-target benchmark comparison is not completely like-for-like.
Section III.A compares 2,468 DR1 anomaly clusters with 2,685 anomalies from a much smaller EDR study and calls the selection “matched on target-class selection.” The models, quality cuts, source populations, thresholds and clustering procedures differ. The comparison can be reported descriptively, but “like-for-like” is too strong.
MINOR — Table III is difficult to interpret and mixes row-based and object-based denominators.
The distinction among full spectra, validated TARGETTYPE subsets, zall-pix rows, deduplicated clusters and matched objects is too complex for a headline table. A flow diagram beginning with unique observations and ending with unique physical sources would be clearer.
MINOR — The Planck patch selection lacks a physical anomaly taxonomy.
Section III.F reports top reconstruction-MSE patches, but there is little characterization of whether they correspond to point sources, foreground residuals, scanning noise, mask edges, non-Gaussian CMB structures or reconstruction artifacts. A CMB anomaly catalog requires null simulations and comparison to Planck noise/foreground products.
MINOR — The spatial correlation tests in Section IV.B are not informative in their present form.
The uniform-per-occupied-pixel χ2\chi^2χ2 null is known in advance to be false because the surveys have strongly varying footprints and targeting densities. Even as a diagnostic, χν2≈15.5\chi_\nu^2\approx15.5χν2​≈15.5 adds little. This section should be removed or replaced by per-survey selection-function-normalized tests.
MINOR — The random-match estimates need geometry-preserving controls.
Section IV.A acknowledges that RA shifts do not preserve survey geometry. The claimed “>60×>60\times>60×” excess of observed multi-survey clusters over random expectation should not be emphasized until rotations or footprint-constrained random catalogs are used.
MINOR — The claimed 95% upper limit on DESI artifact contamination is conditional on a non-random sample.
The 0/200 inspection result applies to the top-ranked 200 objects and to a restricted artifact checklist. It is not an unbiased binomial sample from the 195,829 entries. The stated ≤1.5%\le1.5\%≤1.5% limit must be explicitly restricted to that inspected stratum and inspection definition.
MINOR — The manuscript duplicates limitations and contains internal redundancy.
Section VI.C lists “single architecture” twice, as items (1) and (8). Numerous threshold and count caveats are repeated in the abstract, reader guide, Tables I–II, Sections III and VI, and conclusions. A substantial editorial reduction would improve clarity.
MINOR — Several terminology choices overstate validation.
Terms including “catalog-grade,” “validated,” “genuine novelty,” “real,” “decisive,” and “confirms” are often applied to results that are actually stability checks, restricted-morphology injections, unmatched-source counts, or model-conditional comparisons. These terms should be replaced with narrower descriptions.
MINOR — The claimed novelty relative to prior work needs a more systematic comparison.
The paper states that prior searches were “limited to individual surveys at sub-million scale,” but no comprehensive comparison is given for recent survey-scale unsupervised searches, catalog-level anomaly systems, foundation-model embeddings, or multi-wavelength outlier catalogs. The novelty is primarily computational scale and cross-archive aggregation, not yet demonstrated scientific yield.
MINOR — The paper’s journal fit is questionable.
The main contribution is an astronomical machine-learning catalog, while the PRD-relevant cosmology sections are secondary and presently underdeveloped. In its current form, the work appears better suited to an astronomical methods or catalog journal unless the cosmological analysis is substantially strengthened.
Overall assessment
The manuscript shows unusual transparency about failed runs, synthetic fallback data, irreproducible score axes and weak validation channels. That transparency is valuable. The underlying project may ultimately produce a useful data release, particularly if restricted to clearly selected science targets with fully documented operating characteristics.
At present, however, the principal 268,519-object result conflates arbitrary fixed-count selections, non-science spectra, heterogeneous validation criteria and a geometry-only QA test. The manuscript therefore does not establish that its headline catalog is a scientifically validated anomaly catalog in the sense required for publication. The secondary fNLf_{\rm NL}fNL​ and NANOGrav applications do not compensate for this problem and themselves require major statistical reconstruction.
